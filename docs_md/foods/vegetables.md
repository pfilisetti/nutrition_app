# Vegetables

---

## Spinach

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked recommended
- **Why cook**: Rich in oxalate, which blocks absorption of calcium and iron. Boiling or steaming for even 1 minute reduces oxalate by up to 87%. Cooking also increases antioxidant capacity (net: cooked has higher nutritional value).
- **Safety**: Rich in nitrates — do not reheat spinach more than once (nitrates turn into toxic nitrites at high heat).

**Key nutrients** (per 100g cooked):

| Nutrient | Amount | % Daily Value |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Vitamin A | 470 mcg | ~67% |
| Vitamin C | 10 mg | ~13% |
| Vitamin K | 495 mcg | ~550% |
| Vitamin B2 | 0.236 mg | ~21% |
| Vitamin B6 | 0.242 mg | ~19% |
| Vitamin B9 (folate) | 146 mcg | ~37% |

## Kale (Chou Frisé)

- **Raw vs cooked**: Raw recommended
- **Why raw**: Chopping or chewing kale releases an enzyme that converts glucosinolates into isothiocyanates — anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer compounds. Heat destroys this enzyme.
- **Tip**: Massage raw kale with olive oil for a few minutes to soften before eating.
- **Trade-off**: Cooking reduces vitamin C, antioxidants, and several minerals.
- **Key nutrients**: glucosinolates, vitamin C, calcium, potassium, iron, zinc, magnesium, vitamins A, K, B6, copper, manganese, fiber

## Broccoli

- **Raw vs cooked**: Raw (or carefully prepared before cooking)
- **Why raw**: Glucosinolates are converted to sulforaphane (anti-cancer) by the enzyme myrosinase when chewed or chopped. Heat damages this enzyme.
- **If cooked**: Chop into small pieces, wait at least 30–90 minutes for sulforaphane to develop, then flash stir-fry for 4 minutes → produces 2.8x more sulforaphane than cooking immediately. Steaming retains more vitamin C and glucosinolates than stir-frying.

**Key nutrients** (per 100g cooked):

| Nutrient | Amount | % Daily Value |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Vitamin C | 64.9 mg | ~87% |
| Vitamin K | 78 mcg (per 76g) | ~87% |

## Carrots

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked recommended
- **Why cook**: Beta-carotene absorption is 6.5x greater in stir-fried carrots than raw. Cooking breaks down cell walls and releases carotenoids.
- **Best method**: Steam up to 10 minutes (preserves carotenoids + concentrates potassium). Boiling also preserves carotenoids but reduces polyphenols. Avoid deep frying.

**Key nutrients** (per 1 medium carrot):

| Nutrient | Amount | % Daily Value |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Vitamin A | 500 mcg | ~71% |
| Vitamin K | 8.1 mcg | ~9% |

## Mushrooms

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked (grill or microwave preferred)
- **Why cook**: Enhances antioxidant activity. Pan-frying increases absorbable protein, essential fatty acids, and carbohydrates. Raw or undercooked mushrooms can contain hazardous toxins.
- **Best method**: Grill or microwave to preserve the most nutrients. Boiling and frying reduce antioxidant levels.

**Key nutrients** (per 100g):

| Nutrient | Amount | % Daily Value |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Vitamin B2 | 0.402 mg | ~37% |
| Vitamin B3 | 3.61 mg | ~26% |
| Vitamin B5 | 1.5 mg | ~30% |

- Also contains: fiber, potassium, selenium, copper

## Garlic

- **Raw vs cooked**: Raw recommended
- **Key compound**: Allicin — responsible for garlic's odor and health benefits (heart health, cancer risk reduction). Activated by an enzyme reaction when garlic is cut or crushed, and deactivated by heat.
- **If cooked**: Chop or slice beforehand and wait a few minutes before adding heat — this allows allicin to develop before the enzyme is destroyed.

---

## Onions

- **Raw vs cooked**: Raw recommended
- **Why raw**: Provides thiosulfinates and allicin — antioxidants linked to cardiovascular health, immune function, and disease protection. Heat destroys these compounds.
- **If cooked**: Sauté or bake at low heat for 5 minutes maximum to increase flavonoid levels. Boiling reduces antioxidants.

---

## Tomatoes

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked recommended
- **Why cook**: Lycopene (linked to reduced cancer risk and cardiovascular disease protection) is more bioavailable after cooking. Heat breaks down cell walls, improving absorption.
- **Best method**: Steaming especially increases antioxidant capacity.
- **Trade-off**: Cooking reduces vitamin C, but tomatoes are not a primary source — citrus, strawberries, and bell peppers are better alternatives.
- **Storage**: Store at room temperature (not the fridge) to optimize ripening and increase lycopene levels.

**Key nutrients** (per 100g):

| Nutrient | Amount | % Daily Value |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Vitamin C | 22.8 mg | ~30% |

## Bell Peppers (Poivrons)

- **Raw vs cooked**: Raw recommended
- **Why raw**: Cooking destroys up to 75% of nutrients.
- **If cooked**: Use dry-heat methods (stir-fry or roast) to minimize losses.
- **Key nutrients**: vitamin C (red peppers are one of the best sources), carotenoids, polyphenols, vitamin E

**Key nutrients** (per 1 medium red pepper):

| Nutrient | Amount | % Daily Value |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Vitamin E | 1.9 mg | ~13% |

## Cauliflower

- **Raw vs cooked**: Raw recommended
- **Why raw**: Highest antioxidant losses of all vegetables observed after boiling and microwaving. Same glucosinolate/myrosinase principle as broccoli and kale.
- **If cooked**: Griddle or stir-fry. For maximum sulforaphane, blend or chop into rice-sized pieces and refrigerate before cooking.
- **Key nutrients**: sulforaphane, antioxidants, vitamin C, fiber

---

## Beets

- **Raw vs cooked**: Raw recommended
- **Why raw**: Nitrates (relaxes blood vessels, lowers blood pressure, supports immune and cardiovascular function) and betalains (antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, liver-protective pigments) are water-soluble — boiling washes them away.
- **Safety**: High-nitrate vegetable — do not reheat beets.

---

## Asparagus

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked recommended
- **Why cook**: Cooking increases total antioxidant activity by 16–25%. Beta-carotene rises by 24% and quercetin by 98%. Cooked asparagus produces 3x more antioxidants per spear than raw.
- **Tip**: Don't overcook. Thin asparagus: 1 minute. Thick spears: 4–5 minutes maximum.

**Key nutrients**:

| Nutrient | Amount | % Daily Value |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Vitamin B9 (folate) | 75 mcg | ~19% |

## Potatoes

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked (then cooled for best glycemic response)
- **Why cook**: Raw potatoes contain resistant starch that is difficult to digest and can cause gastrointestinal issues. Cooking makes them safe and digestible.
- **Blood sugar tip**: Potatoes spike blood sugar due to high starch content. Best served cooked then cooled or in a vinaigrette — converts digestible starch to resistant starch (retrogradation), reducing glycemic load from 90 to 56%.
- **Key nutrients**: vitamin C, B6, folate, iron, potassium, fiber

## Celery

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked for antioxidants; raw for fiber and vitamin C
- **Why cook**: Boiling, pressure-cooking, baking, grilling, and frying all increase antioxidant levels. Boiling also makes folate more available.
- **Trade-off**: Cooking destroys fiber and reduces vitamin C.
- **Safety**: High-nitrate vegetable — do not reheat celery.
- **Key nutrients**: antioxidants, fiber, folate, potassium, vitamins A, C, K

---

## Green Beans (Haricots Verts)

- **Raw vs cooked**: Cooked recommended
- **Why cook**: Raw green beans contain lectin, an antinutrient that disrupts digestion and blocks absorption of calcium, iron, phosphorus, and zinc. High heat or water-based cooking inactivates lectins. Cooking also increases availability of certain antioxidants.
