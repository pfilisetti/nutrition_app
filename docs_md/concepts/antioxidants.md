# Antioxidants

## What Are Antioxidants?

Antioxidants are molecules that fight harmful free radicals in the body. They donate an electron to a free radical without becoming unstable themselves, stabilizing the free radical and reducing its reactivity.

**Free radicals** are oxygen-containing molecules with an unpaired electron that react easily with other molecules, causing chain reactions called oxidation. They are linked to diabetes, heart disease, and cancer — but also serve important functions (e.g., immune cells use free radicals to fight infections).

When free radicals outnumber antioxidants, the result is **oxidative stress**, which damages DNA and other important molecules and can lead to cell death.

The body generates its own antioxidants (e.g., glutathione) and also obtains them from food.

---

## Key Sources of Antioxidants

- Berries, green tea, coffee, dark chocolate
- Red cabbage, beans, beets, spinach
- Spices: ginger, turmeric, garlic
- Herbs: rosemary, parsley, sage
- Note: Antioxidants are also used as food additives to increase shelf life (e.g., vitamin C added to processed foods as a preservative)

**Important**: Foods reduce oxidative damage more effectively than isolated supplements. Food compounds work synergistically — taking one or two isolated nutrients does not have the same beneficial effect. Excessive intake of isolated antioxidants can paradoxically promote oxidative damage ("antioxidant paradox").

## Types of Antioxidants

### Water-soluble vs Fat-soluble
- **Water-soluble** antioxidants act in the fluid inside and outside cells (e.g., vitamin C)
- **Fat-soluble** antioxidants act primarily in cell membranes (e.g., vitamin E)

### Endogenous vs Exogenous
- **Endogenous**: produced by the body (e.g., glutathione)
- **Exogenous**: obtained from diet

## Flavonoids

A major family of antioxidants, part of the broader polyphenol group. Over 9,000 different flavonoids have been identified. Flavonoids represent about two-thirds of total dietary phenolics consumed.

Six types found in food:
- **Flavanols**: may help manage symptoms of cardiovascular disease
- **Flavan-3-ols**: can reduce risks associated with cardiovascular diseases
- **Flavones**: may help with inflammation; pigments in blue and white flowering plants
- **Flavanones**: anti-inflammatory; may help manage weight and cholesterol
- **Isoflavones**: (found in soy and legumes)
- **Anthocyanins**: may safeguard against type 2 diabetes; give red, purple, and blue color to fruits and vegetables; naturally produced pigments used as food colorants

Note: Anthocyanins are quickly metabolized and eliminated — less than 1.8% of consumed anthocyanins are normally absorbed (poor bioavailability).

## Polyphenols

4 categories:
- **Flavonoids** (~60% of all polyphenols): quercetin, kaempferol, catechins, anthocyanins — found in apples, onions, dark chocolate, red cabbage
- **Phenolic acids** (~30%): ferulic and chlorogenic acids — found in coffee and cereal grains
- **Polyphenolic amides**: capsaicinoids (chili peppers), avenanthramides (oats)
- **Other polyphenols**: stilbenes (grapes, berries), resveratrol (red wine), ellagic acid (berries), curcumin (turmeric), lignans (flax seeds, sesame seeds, whole grains), oleocanthal (extra virgin olive oil — anti-inflammatory)

## Carotenoids (Pro-Vitamin A)

- Act as antioxidants in the body
- **Lutein and zeaxanthin**: protect the eyes from oxygen free radicals produced by light exposure; reduce risk of cataracts and macular degeneration
- Beta-carotene is converted to vitamin A in the body

## Antioxidant Enzymes (Mineral-Dependent)

### Selenium
- Essential component of antioxidant enzymes, especially GPX (glutathione peroxidase)
- Selenoproteins trap H2O2, controlling oxidative signals at the cellular level

### Manganese, Copper, Zinc
- Components of superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzymes:
  - SOD1 (copper + zinc): found in cytosol and mitochondrial intermembrane space
  - SOD2 (manganese): found in mitochondrial matrix and inner membrane
  - SOD3 (extracellular superoxide dismutase)

### Sulfur
- Key component of glutathione, the body's primary endogenous antioxidant

## Factors That Promote Oxidative Stress (Free Radical Overload)

- High blood sugar levels
- Environmental pollution
- Excessive intake of iron, magnesium, copper, or zinc
- Sleep deprivation
- Overeating
- Intense or prolonged exercise (the body produces free radicals as a by-product of energy metabolism during exercise)
