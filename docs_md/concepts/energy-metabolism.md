# Energy Metabolism

## Energy Sources

The body's primary energy source is glucose. When glucose is unavailable (low carb intake, fasting, diabetes), the body shifts to burning fat, producing ketones as an alternative fuel.

**Priority order**: Glucose → Glycogen stores → Fat (ketones)

---

## Ketosis

Ketosis occurs when the body gets more energy from fat than from glucose:
1. Glucose is insufficient (low carb intake, fasting, or insulin deficiency)
2. Insulin levels drop → fatty acids released from fat stores in large amounts
3. Fatty acids transported to the liver → oxidized into ketones (ketone bodies)
4. Ketones used as alternative energy source throughout the body

Ketosis is a **normal metabolic state** — not inherently dangerous. The body can function on ketones.

In healthy people without diabetes, insulin and glucagon maintain steady glucose levels and prevent excessive ketone buildup.

---

## Ketoacidosis (DKA — Diabetic Ketoacidosis)

DKA is a dangerous condition distinct from normal ketosis:
- Occurs in people with diabetes (especially type 1) when insulin levels are critically low
- Ketones accumulate faster than the body can use or excrete them
- Blood becomes acidic (ketones are acidic) → life-threatening if untreated
- **Key distinction**: Normal ketosis = controlled, regulated ketone production. DKA = uncontrolled, excessive accumulation.

## Triglycerides

- The body's main form of stored fat energy
- When you eat more calories than you need, the excess is converted to triglycerides and stored in fat cells
- Different from cholesterol: triglycerides = energy storage; cholesterol = cell building and hormone production
- Transported through the bloodstream via lipoproteins
- High triglyceride levels are associated with consuming more calories than burned, high refined carbohydrate intake, and excess alcohol

## Hormones That Regulate Energy and Weight

| Hormone | Role |
|---------|------|
| **Insulin** | Signals cells to absorb blood glucose; regulates macronutrient metabolism; inhibits muscle protein breakdown |
| **Glucagon** | Signals liver to release stored glycogen as glucose when blood sugar is low |
| **Leptin** | Appetite suppressor; high levels = satiety + energy expenditure |
| **Ghrelin** | Appetite increaser; high levels = hunger and fat conservation |
| **T3 (Triiodothyronine)** | Regulates metabolic rate |

**Important**: People who lose weight experience higher ghrelin levels (more hunger) and lower energy — a biological response that can lead to diet abandonment and weight regain. This is why sustainable caloric deficits are more effective than extreme restriction.

---

## Weight Loss

Weight loss requires a **caloric deficit** — no exceptions.

**Two stages**:
1. **Early stage**: Rapid weight loss, mostly water. Low-carb diets especially cause significant water loss (less glycogen stored = less water retained).
2. **Later stage**: Slower, steady fat loss over a longer period.

**Healthy weekly target**: ~0.7% of body weight per week — maximizes fat loss while minimizing metabolic adaptation and muscle loss.

**Sustainable deficit**: 500 calories below maintenance per day for weight loss.

**Low-carb diets**: Tend to produce faster initial weight loss than low-fat diets (faster glycogen and water depletion), but long-term outcomes are similar.

## Glycogen: Muscle Fuel

Glycogen is glucose stored primarily in the liver and muscles:
- **Liver glycogen**: Regulates blood glucose levels; replenishes blood glucose during exercise and fasting; depleted after 12–24 hours of fasting
- **Muscle glycogen**: Primary fuel for muscle contractions during exercise; ~75% of total body glycogen is in skeletal muscle
- Occupies 1–2% of skeletal muscle cell volume; 5–6% of liver cell volume

Glycogen depletion during exercise:
- Rate of depletion is proportional to exercise intensity
- At >60% VO2max, blood glucose and muscle glycogen are the primary fuels
- High-intensity exercise (e.g., repeated sprinting) can rapidly empty glycogen stores even in short sessions

Replenishment: Consuming sufficient carbohydrates after exercise restores muscle glycogen.
