# Minerals

## General Principles

Essential minerals are classified into two groups:

**Major minerals** (used and stored in large quantities): calcium, chloride, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, sulfur

**Trace minerals** (vital but needed in small amounts): chromium, copper, fluoride, iodine, iron, manganese, molybdenum, selenium, zinc

---

## Calcium

- **Daily intake**: 1,000 mg
- **Functions**: Builds bones and teeth; allows normal body movement by keeping tissue rigid, strong, and flexible
- **Note**: Most abundant mineral in the human body (1.5–2% of body weight); over 99% is in bones. Requires vitamin D for absorption in the gut. Calcium from dairy is more easily absorbed than from plant sources. Calcium can slow both heme and non-heme iron absorption. Deficiency is common.

---

## Phosphorus

- **Daily intake**: 700 mg
- **Functions**: Essential for bone calcification (along with calcium — 85% of body phosphorus is in the skeleton); regulates gene transcription, enzyme activation, pH maintenance, and intracellular energy storage
- **Note**: Phosphorus in seeds and unleavened breads is in the form of phytic acid — human intestines lack the phytase enzyme needed to absorb it, so much is unavailable

---

## Magnesium

- **Daily intake**: 310 mg
- **Functions**: Cofactor in over 300 enzyme systems; regulates protein synthesis, muscle and nerve function, blood glucose control, and blood pressure; required for energy production (oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis); required for DNA, RNA, and glutathione synthesis; may help muscle recovery, depression, and PMS symptoms
- **Note**: 60–65% found in bone; 27% in muscles. Also found in tap, mineral, and bottled water (1–120 mg/L). Deficiency is very common — ~70% of US adults under 71 and ~80% over 71 consume less than the required amount. Magnesium deficiency can cause potassium deficiency (kidneys need magnesium to reabsorb potassium).

---

## Potassium

- **Daily intake**: 3,833 mg (optimal); minimum ~833 mg; max 4,700 mg
- **Functions**: Sends nerve impulses; releases energy from fat, carbohydrates, and protein; maintains intracellular fluid volume; transmembrane electrochemical gradients; manages blood pressure by relaxing blood vessels and excreting sodium; helps muscles contract
- **Note**: Strong relationship with sodium — the sodium-to-potassium ratio (Na:K) is a better predictor of hypertension than either alone. Recommended Na:K ratio: ≤0.6 mg/mg. Current average: 3,400 mg sodium vs 2,500 mg potassium (ratio of 1.36). People with the highest potassium intakes had a 20% lower risk of death. Deficiency is extremely common — less than 0.015% of American adults meet daily needs.

---

## Sodium

- **Daily intake**: Under 2,300 mg/day (one teaspoon); minimum needed: 500 mg
- **Functions**: Balances fluids; sends nerve impulses; makes muscles contract; main regulator of extracellular fluid volume
- **Warning**: Excess sodium causes the body to retain water, increases blood volume, stiffens blood vessels, and leads to high blood pressure, heart attack, and stroke. People with the highest sodium intakes had a 20% higher risk of death from any cause. ~75% of dietary sodium comes from processed foods. 9 out of 10 US adults will develop hypertension at some point.
- **Tip**: Taste buds don't notice a sodium reduction of ~30%. Combining citrus with food can reduce sodium needed, as they activate the same taste sensors.

---

## Iron

- **Daily intake**: 18 mg
- **Functions**: Makes hemoglobin and myoglobin; supports muscle metabolism and healthy connective tissue; physical growth; neurological development; cellular function; hormone synthesis; energy efficiency
- **Note**: Most iron (3–4 g) is in hemoglobin. Stored as ferritin/hemosiderin in liver, spleen, and bone marrow, or as myoglobin in muscle. Transported in blood by transferrin. Deficiency progresses: iron store depletion → iron-deficiency erythropoiesis → iron-deficiency anemia (IDA). Calcium can slow iron absorption. Higher training intensity may increase iron needs. Deficiency is common.

---

## Zinc

- **Daily intake**: 8 mg
- **Functions**: Blood clotting; protein and DNA synthesis; immune system support; wound healing; cell division; sense of taste
- **Note**: Stored mostly in skeletal muscle and bone

---

## Magnesium (see above)

---

## Copper

- **Daily intake**: 900 mcg (0.9 mg)
- **Functions**: Metabolizes fuel; makes red blood cells; regulates neurotransmitters; mops up free radicals; cofactor for cuproenzymes involved in energy production, iron metabolism, neuropeptide activation, connective tissue synthesis, and neurotransmitter synthesis; brain development; immune function; gene expression regulation
- **Note**: Almost two-thirds of body copper is in skeleton and muscle. Typical US diets meet or exceed the RDA.

---

## Manganese

- **Daily intake**: 1.8 mg
- **Functions**: Forms bones; metabolizes amino acids, glucose, cholesterol, and carbohydrates; bone formation; immune responses; antioxidant (part of superoxide dismutase, SOD2); blood clotting with vitamin K
- **Note**: 25–40% in bone. Men absorb dietary manganese less efficiently than women, possibly due to higher iron status. Iron and manganese share an intestinal transporter, so high iron intake reduces manganese absorption.

---

## Selenium

- **Daily intake**: 55 mcg
- **Functions**: Critical roles in thyroid hormone metabolism, DNA synthesis, reproduction; protection from oxidative damage and infection; key component of antioxidant enzymes (GPX, selenoproteins)
- **Note**: ~28–46% of total body selenium is in skeletal muscle

---

## Iodine

- **Daily intake**: 150 mcg
- **Functions**: Essential component of thyroid hormones T4 and T3; regulates protein synthesis, enzymatic activity, and metabolic rate
- **Note**: Added to iodized salt. Deficiency leads to goiter (enlarged thyroid). Deficiency is common in regions with iodine-poor soils. Deficiency common globally.

---

## Sulfur

- **Daily intake**: N/A (no RDA established)
- **Functions**: Making proteins; regulating gene expression; building and repairing DNA; metabolizing food; essential for making and recycling glutathione (main antioxidant); maintains integrity of connective tissue (skin, tendons, ligaments)
- **Note**: Third most abundant mineral in the body (after calcium and phosphorus). Derived almost exclusively from proteins — only 2 of 20 amino acids contain sulfur: methionine and cysteine. Most protein-containing foods contain some methionine.

---

## Chloride

- **Daily intake**: (follows sodium intake)
- **Functions**: Regulates fluid and nutrient flow in and out of cells; maintains pH levels; stimulates stomach acid for digestion; supports nerve and muscle cell action; facilitates oxygen and carbon dioxide flow within cells
- **Note**: Usually bound to sodium — blood levels coincide with sodium levels. Most foods containing sodium also provide chloride.

---

## Chromium

- **Daily intake**: N/A
- **Functions**: May help maintain normal blood sugar levels; helps cells draw energy from blood sugar
- **Note**: Not classified as an essential mineral since its absence doesn't produce abnormalities reversible by adding chromium. Trivalent chromium (III) found in food is non-toxic; hexavalent chromium (VI) from industrial pollution is toxic and carcinogenic.

---

## Fluoride

- **Daily intake**: 3 mg
- **Functions**: Inhibits and reverses dental decay; stimulates new bone formation
- **Note**: Bones and teeth store ~99% of body fluoride. Ionic form of the element fluorine. Primarily comes from fluoridated water, food/beverages prepared with it, and dental products.

---

## Molybdenum

- **Daily intake**: 45 mcg
- **Functions**: Activates enzymes that break down toxins; prevents buildup of harmful sulfites; required for 4 enzymes (sulfite oxidase, xanthine oxidase, aldehyde oxidase, mARC)
- **Note**: Deficiency is very rare. Present in soil and transferred to plants. Estimated average US intake exceeds the RDA.
