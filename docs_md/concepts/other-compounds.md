# Other Compounds

## Cholesterol

### What Is Cholesterol?
Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance (lipid) made primarily by the liver. It travels through the bloodstream packaged in protein-covered particles called **lipoproteins**. The body needs cholesterol to:
- Make hormones (including sex hormones and cortisol)
- Produce vitamin D
- Produce digestive bile acids
- Form cell membrane layers

Only ~20% of blood cholesterol comes from food — the body produces the rest. If you eat 200–300mg of dietary cholesterol, your liver adds ~800mg more from fat, sugars, and proteins.

### Lipoprotein Types (5 main types)

| Type | Role |
|------|------|
| **Chylomicrons** | Large particles; carry dietary triglycerides from digestion |
| **VLDL** (Very Low Density) | Made by liver; carry triglycerides to tissues |
| **IDL** (Intermediate Density) | Transition form as VLDLs give up fatty acids → some cleared by liver, rest become LDL |
| **LDL** (Low Density) — "bad" | Rich in cholesterol; delivers to tissues; associated with artery-clogging plaque buildup |
| **HDL** (High Density) — "good" | Removes excess cholesterol from arteries, returns it to liver for excretion |

### LDL (Bad Cholesterol)
- Caused by: red meat, full-fat dairy, saturated fats, trans fats, processed foods, lack of exercise
- Builds waxy plaque in arteries (atherosclerosis) → can rupture → blood clots → heart attack or stroke
- LDL has 3 subclasses (A, B, I) — subclass B is the most damaging and a better heart attack predictor than total LDL
- Raised by: long-chain saturated fats, trans fats, refined carbohydrates

### HDL (Good Cholesterol)
- Acts like a vacuum cleaner — removes cholesterol and plaque from arteries, sends it to the liver
- Represents ~1/4 to 1/3 of blood cholesterol
- Raised by: unsaturated fats, exercise

### Triglycerides
- The body's main form of fat storage — calories not immediately used are converted to triglycerides
- High levels associated with excess calorie intake, especially refined carbs
- Transported in blood by lipoproteins; raised by excess sugar and alcohol

### How to Lower LDL
The most important lever is **overall diet quality**, not just avoiding dietary cholesterol:
- Replace saturated and trans fats with healthy unsaturated fats (vegetable oils, avocados, fatty fish)
- Replace refined carbs (white bread, pasta, white rice) with whole grains — they're low in fiber, which is critical for cholesterol management
- **Soluble fiber** lowers LDL by: (1) trapping cholesterol for excretion, (2) binding bile acids → forces liver to use blood cholesterol to make more bile acids → lowers LDL

## Choline

- Essential nutrient; water-soluble; not a vitamin or mineral, but often grouped with B vitamins
- **Daily intake**: 425 mg
- The body produces some choline, but not enough — diet is required to avoid deficiency; most people fall short

**Functions**:
- Synthesizes phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin (phospholipids critical for cell membrane integrity)
- Produces acetylcholine (neurotransmitter for memory, mood, muscle control, brain and nervous system function)
- Modulates gene expression, cell membrane signaling, lipid transport and metabolism
- Supports early brain development
- When folate (B9) is deficient, choline becomes the primary methyl donor — increasing choline needs

**Deficiency effects**: Muscle damage, liver damage, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)

## Antinutrients

Antinutrients are substances in food that interfere with digestion and absorption of other nutrients. The main ones are phytic acid and lectins.

**Note on iron**: Heme iron (animal sources) absorbs efficiently; non-heme iron (plant sources) absorbs poorly and is highly affected by phytic acid. Zinc is also well absorbed from meat even in the presence of phytic acid. Antinutrients are mainly a concern for vegetarians, vegans, or those at risk of iron/zinc deficiency.

### Phytic Acid (Phytate / IP6)
- Found in plant seeds (grains, legumes, nuts)
- Reduces absorption of iron, zinc, and calcium from the food it's in (does NOT affect absorption from other foods eaten hours later)
- Has health benefits too: antioxidant properties (used commercially as a food preservative); may protect against insulin resistance
- **How to reduce**: Soaking (overnight in water), sprouting, fermentation — all significantly reduce phytic acid content
- For most people on a balanced diet, not a concern. Greater concern for vegetarians and vegans.

### Lectins
- Family of carbohydrate-binding proteins found in almost all foods, especially legumes and grains
- Some plant lectins are toxic (e.g., ricin from castor oil plant — lethal)
- In practice: lectin-rich foods are almost always cooked beforehand, leaving negligible amounts
- **How to reduce**: Soaking (5+ hours), sprouting, fermentation, and boiling all significantly reduce lectin content
- Dried beans: soak at least 5 hours, then boil in fresh water for 30 minutes

## Organic Acids

Organic compounds with acidic properties, classified by number of carboxylic functions. They are intermediates in the metabolic degradation of amino acids, fats, and carbohydrates.
