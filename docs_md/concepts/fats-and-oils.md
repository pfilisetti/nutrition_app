# Fats and Oils

## Types of Dietary Fat

All fats are made of fatty acids attached to a glycerol molecule. They vary by chain length (short, medium, long) and degree of saturation (number of double bonds). Chain length determines how the body metabolizes them and how efficiently they provide energy.

**Total fat intake**: should not exceed 35% of daily calories; max 11% from saturated fat (~70g total / 20g saturated for an average adult woman on 2,000 kcal)

### Reading Fat Labels (per 100g)

| Level | Total Fat | Saturated Fat |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| High | > 17.5g | > 5g |
| Medium | 3.1–17.5g | — |
| Low | < 3g | < 1.5g |

**Key principle**: Fat is essential — it builds cell membranes, makes nerve tissue and hormones, and aids absorption of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K. Low fat intake limits these critical functions.

## Saturated Fats

- Saturated with hydrogen molecules; contain only single bonds between carbons — tightly packed
- Solid at room temperature
- More stable for cooking than unsaturated fats
- Not all saturated fats are equal — effects depend on chain length:
  - **Short-chain** (< 6 carbons): by-products of fiber fermentation in the gut (butyric, propionic, acetic acid)
  - **Medium-chain** (6–12 carbons): metabolized differently — go straight to the liver, used as instant energy or converted to ketones; harder to store as fat; found in coconut oil, palm oil, dairy
  - **Long-chain** (12–22+ carbons): most of the saturated fat we eat; some raise LDL cholesterol
    - **Stearic acid**: does not raise heart disease risk
    - **Palmitic acid**: raises total and LDL cholesterol; most common saturated fatty acid (over half of all saturated fat eaten in the US); found in palm oil, red meat, dairy
    - **Myristic acid**: raises LDL more than other fatty acids

### Fatty Acid Chain Lengths (Common Saturated Fatty Acids)

| Fatty Acid | Carbon Chain |
|-----------|-------------|
| Stearic acid | 18C |
| Palmitic acid | 16C |
| Myristic acid | 14C |
| Lauric acid | 12C |
| Capric acid | 10C |
| Caprylic acid | 8C |
| Caproic acid | 6C |

**Bottom line**: Saturated fat is not uniformly bad. Compared to trans fats → safer. Compared to whole grains → neutral. Compared to refined carbs → often a better choice. Dairy fermented products (cheese, kefir, yogurt) may be less harmful than other saturated fat sources.

## Unsaturated Fats

- Held together by double bonds — loosely packed
- Liquid at room temperature
- More vulnerable to damage at high heat

### MUFAs (Monounsaturated Fatty Acids)
- One double bond
- Good for heart health; lower cardiovascular disease risk; more stable than PUFAs when heated
- Found in: olive oil, avocado oil, rapeseed oil, almonds, cashews, hazelnuts, peanuts, pistachios, avocados

### PUFAs (Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids)
- Multiple double bonds — more vulnerable to oxidation at high heat
- Essential: the body cannot produce them; must come from diet
- Biologically active in blood clotting and inflammation

**Omega-3** (anti-inflammatory):
- **ALA** (18 carbons): plant-based; most common dietary omega-3; converted to EPA/DHA inefficiently (~5%); found in flax seeds, chia seeds, walnuts
- **EPA** (20 carbons): marine-based; forms anti-inflammatory eicosanoids; particularly effective against depression; found in fatty fish, seafood, fish oil
- **DHA** (22 carbons): marine-based; structural component of nerve cell membranes (~40% of brain PUFAs, >90% of brain omega-3s); critical for eye and brain function; found in fatty fish, seafood, algae, fish oil
- Recommended: 250–500 mg combined EPA+DHA/day; two portions of fatty fish/week (one 140g oily fish)
- Benefits: reduces triglycerides, depression, anxiety, ADHD symptoms, belly fat, fatty liver, dementia risk; improves eye health

**Omega-6** (pro-inflammatory in excess):
- Linoleic acid is the most common form
- Found in corn oil, sunflower oil, nuts, seeds
- Western diets have a 15–17:1 omega-6 to omega-3 ratio; ideal is 1:1 to 4:1
- Reduce omega-6 intake rather than simply adding more omega-3 to compensate

**Omega-9** (MUFA, non-essential — body can produce):
- Oleic acid is the most common; also the most common MUFA in the diet
- Benefits: less inflammation, better insulin sensitivity
- Found in olive oil, avocado oil, rapeseed oil

## Trans Fats

- Man-made (industrial) fats created by hydrogenating vegetable oils
- No nutritional value; worst fat type for cardiovascular health
- Raise LDL, lower HDL, increase triglycerides → coronary heart disease risk
- Linked to increased cancer risk, brain and nervous system damage
- Found in: processed biscuits, cakes, pies, takeaway foods
- How to avoid: cook from scratch; check labels for "hydrogenated," "partially hydrogenated," "semi-hydrogenated"

**Natural trans fats (ruminant)**: found in meat and dairy of cows, sheep, goats — safe in moderation
- Best known: **CLA (Conjugated Linoleic Acid)** — found in meat and dairy of grass-fed animals; 300–500% higher in grass-fed vs grain-fed beef/dairy; 28 different forms; may help reduce type 2 diabetes and cancer risk; linked to weight loss (though evidence mixed); large doses in supplement form may cause liver fat accumulation

---

## Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs)

- Produced when gut bacteria ferment fiber in the colon
- Most important: butyric acid, propionic acid, acetic acid
- Butyric acid is the primary energy source for colon cells; may prevent colorectal cancer
- Health benefits: reduce colon pH, support gut bacteria, reduce inflammation
- Note: butyrate supplements are absorbed in the small intestine before reaching the colon — lose their benefits for colon cells

## Cooking Oils Guide

### How to Choose an Oil
- **Unrefined / virgin / cold-pressed**: minimally processed; retains nutrients, enzymes, polyphenols; lower smoke point
- **Refined**: processed with chemicals; higher smoke point; fewer nutrients; longer shelf life; may contain chemical residues

**General rule**: The more tightly packed the fatty acids, the more stable the oil at high heat. Saturated and MUFA-rich oils handle heat better than PUFA-rich oils.

---

### Olive Oil
- **Fat type**: MUFA (oleic acid dominant)
- **Smoke point**: 160–190°C (unrefined)
- **Why use**: Oleic acid is 50x less likely to oxidize than linoleic acid (PUFA in sunflower oil). EVOO has the lowest oxidation rate of any cooking oil. Rich in polyphenols and vitamin E (antioxidants).
- **Benefits**: Protects against heart disease, reduces type 2 diabetes risk; improves overall fat quality of diet
- **Best use**: Low to medium heat cooking; cold use (dressings, dipping)

### Avocado Oil
- **Fat type**: MUFA-rich; also contains carotenoids, lutein, zeaxanthin, vitamins A and E
- **Smoke point**: 190–205°C (unrefined) — higher than olive oil
- **Benefits**: Cardiovascular health; eye health (lutein/zeaxanthin); skin membrane health
- **Best use**: Medium to high heat cooking

### Rapeseed Oil (Canola / Huile de Colza)
- **Fat type**: High MUFA, half the saturated fat of olive oil; good omega-6:omega-3 ratio (2:1)
- **Why use**: Cold-pressed version has superior fatty acid profile, high polyphenols, carotenoids, and vitamin E; rich in ALA (omega-3)
- **Best use**: Cold-pressed for cold use or light cooking; avoid chemically refined versions

### Ghee
- **Fat type**: Saturated; high smoke point; lactose-free
- **Smoke point**: High (180°C+)
- **Why use**: Creates fewer damaging acrylamides than vegetable oils at high temperatures; long shelf life
- **Best use**: High-heat cooking, frying

### Coconut Oil
- **Fat type**: 92% saturated; high in MCTs (medium-chain triglycerides)
- **Smoke point**: Relatively low despite high saturation — avoid for deep frying
- **Note**: More resistant to oxidation than sunflower/olive oil due to high saturation, but low smoke point limits high-heat use. Best used sparingly due to high saturated fat content and lack of essential fatty acids (linoleic and linolenic).
- **Best use**: Sautéing and baking at moderate temperatures only

### Sunflower Oil
- **Fat type**: Low saturated fat; high PUFA (linoleic acid) or MUFA (oleic acid, in high-oleic version)
- **Smoke point**: 107–160°C (unrefined)
- **Warning**: Generates more damaging aldehydes than olive, rapeseed, or coconut oil regardless of cooking method. Recommended for low-heat cooking only.
- **Best use**: Cold use or very low heat

### Red Palm Oil
- **Fat type**: High saturated fat (medium-chain variety); no trans fats; retains carotenoids and vitamin E (unlike refined palm kernel oil)
- **Benefits**: May reduce atherosclerosis and high blood pressure risk; stable at high temperatures
- **Note**: Not to be confused with palm kernel oil (refined, stripped of antioxidants, used in processed foods)
- **Best use**: High-heat cooking (frying, roasting) — use sparingly

### Sesame Oil
- **Fat type**: Rich in anti-inflammatory and antioxidant compounds
- **Best use**: Finishing oil or low-heat cooking; strong flavor

### Peanut Oil (Huile d'Arachide)
- **Fat type**: 50% MUFA (oleic acid), 30% PUFA; good source of vitamin E (unrefined)
- **Smoke point**: 160°C (unrefined)
- **Note**: High PUFA content means cooking times should be kept short

### Grapeseed Oil
- **Fat type**: High PUFA (linoleic acid) and vitamin E
- **Note**: Few health studies exist; may benefit cardiovascular health and insulin management. Assume chemical solvent extraction unless stated otherwise.

### Vegetable / Seed Oils
- **Fat type**: High PUFA, especially omega-6
- **Warning**: High omega-6 content; susceptible to oxidation; damaged easily at high temperatures and with repeated use. Often extracted with chemical solvents and may contain additives (e.g., E900 anti-foaming agent).
- **Best use**: Avoid for cooking; consider healthier alternatives
