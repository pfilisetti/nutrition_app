# Weight Loss and Muscle Gain

## Muscle Basics

- ~30% of adults over 70 have trouble with walking, getting up from a chair, or climbing stairs
- **Sarcopenia**: age-related loss of muscle mass and strength — a major cause of physical decline
- Muscle mass and strength peak at age 30–35, then decline slowly; faster decline after age 65 (women) and 70 (men)
- Best prevention: combination of walking and resistance training

### Types of Muscle Hypertrophy
- **Myofibrillar hypertrophy**: growth of muscle contraction components → more strength and speed
- **Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy**: increased muscle glycogen storage → more sustained energy for endurance

---

## Training: General Principles

**Resistance training** (strength training) is distinct from aerobic exercise (running, cycling, walking).

Types: weightlifting (machines or free weights), medicine balls, resistance bands, bodyweight exercises (pushups, squats, yoga).

**How it works**: Muscles contract against gravity → burn through ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency) → metabolic and chemical cascade replenishes ATP → short-term changes in muscle DNA increase protein production for sugar and fat metabolism.

At exercise intensities above 60% VO2max, blood glucose and muscle glycogen are the primary fuels.

---

## Training: Weight Lifting for Muscle Growth

To build muscle, you need both **mechanical damage** and **metabolic fatigue**:
- **Mechanical damage**: heavy weight forces contractile proteins to generate force → structural damage to muscle fibers → repair response → increased muscle size
- **Metabolic fatigue**: muscle fibers exhaust available ATP → can no longer sustain contractions

**Repetition continuum** (general guideline):
- 1–5 reps: builds more strength
- 8–12 reps: builds more muscle mass
- 15+ reps: builds muscular endurance

You don't need to train to complete "failure" to see results.

## Muscle Fuel: Glycogen

Glycogen is glucose stored in the liver and muscles. It is the primary fuel for muscle contractions during exercise.

- ~75% of total body glycogen is stored in skeletal muscles
- Liver glycogen: regulates blood glucose; starts breaking down at the start of exercise to maintain blood glucose; almost fully depleted after 12–24 hours of fasting
- Muscle glycogen: main energy source for active muscles during exercise; depletion rate is proportional to exercise intensity
- High-intensity exercise (e.g., repeated sprinting) rapidly depletes muscle glycogen even in short sessions

**Replenishment**: Consuming sufficient carbohydrates after exercise restores glycogen. Insulin and glucagon are the primary hormones regulating blood glucose and glycogen conversion.

## Nutrition for Muscle Building

### Protein
- Build and maintain muscle with a daily intake of **1.4–2.0 g protein/kg body weight**
- Aim for 20–30g protein per meal
- Focus on whole food protein sources containing all essential amino acids (EAAs)
- Prioritize foods rich in **leucine** — the amino acid most directly linked to muscle protein synthesis
- Muscle will be lost if the body removes more protein from muscle than it adds

### Calories for Muscle Gain
- Eat **300–500 calories above daily maintenance** for sustainable muscle gain without excessive fat gain
- Your body has a maximum muscle-building rate — excess calories beyond that are stored as fat
- Realistic muscle gain: **0.25–0.9 kg (0.5–2 lbs) per month** in adult males
- An average active man needs ~2,600 cal/day to maintain weight; an active woman ~2,000 cal/day

### Fat Intake During Muscle Building
- Consume **0.5–1.5 g fat/kg body weight** per day
- Limit high-fat proteins: fatty cuts of pork/beef, bacon, chicken wings/thighs, fatty fish (during cutting)

## Bulking and Cutting

### Bulking
- Eating more calories than burned to support muscle growth
- Unavoidable: some fat gain will occur alongside muscle gain
- Focus on caloric surplus (300–500 cal above maintenance), adequate protein, and resistance training

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Maximizes muscle growth | Gains fat alongside muscle |
| Supports high training intensity | Requires a subsequent cutting phase |
| Allows progressive overload | May feel sluggish or heavy |

### Cutting
- Restricting calories to reduce body fat while preserving muscle
- General rule: 500 calories below maintenance to stimulate weight loss
- Resistance training remains important during cutting, though intensity may be lower (less energy available)
- Maintain protein intake to minimize muscle loss

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Reduces body fat | Risk of losing some muscle mass |
| Improves muscle definition | Lower energy → reduced training intensity |
| Improves cardiovascular health markers | Harder to maintain — ghrelin increases hunger |

### Cycling Bulking and Cutting
Alternating bulking and cutting cycles allows gradual muscle gain while preventing excessive fat accumulation:
1. Bulk: build muscle (and some fat)
2. Cut: lose the fat while retaining the muscle
3. Repeat at a higher baseline

## Weight Loss Fundamentals

- **Weight loss cannot occur without a caloric deficit**
- Target: ~0.7% of body weight per week — maximizes fat loss while minimizing metabolic adaptation and muscle loss
- Avoid extreme caloric deficits: hormonal responses (increased ghrelin, increased fatigue) undermine adherence

### Weight Loss Stages
1. **Early stage**: Rapid weight loss, mostly water. Low-carb diets especially deplete glycogen → less water retained
2. **Later stage**: Slower, steady fat loss

### Hormones Affecting Weight Loss
- **Leptin**: increases with body fat → suppresses appetite and increases energy expenditure
- **Ghrelin**: increases during weight loss → triggers hunger and fat conservation → primary reason diets are hard to maintain. **Warning**: If a caloric deficit is too aggressive or sustained too long, ghrelin rebound can cause the body to preferentially burn lean mass (muscle) instead of fat, undermining body composition even if weight is lost.
- **T3**: influences metabolic rate
- **Insulin**: regulates macronutrient metabolism; inhibits muscle protein breakdown
