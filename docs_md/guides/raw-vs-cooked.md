# Raw vs Cooked: Nutrient Guide

## General Principles

Nutrients most affected by cooking:
- **Water-soluble vitamins**: C, B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), B5, B6, B9 (folate), B12
- **Fat-soluble vitamins**: A, D, E, K (less affected overall)
- **Minerals**: potassium, magnesium, sodium, calcium

Tips to maximize nutrient retention:
- Don't peel vegetables until after cooking
- Eat cooked vegetables within 1–2 days (vitamin C continues to decline when exposed to air)
- Steaming and light pan-frying are generally the best cooking methods
- Grilling, microwaving, and baking are also good
- Boiling and deep frying over long periods cause the most nutrient loss
- Eating vegetables with a little oil increases absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)

---

## Spinach

- **Recommended**: Cooked
- **Key nutrients**: vitamin C, magnesium, B6, iron, calcium
- **Why cook**: Spinach is rich in oxalate, which blocks absorption of calcium and iron. Boiling or steaming for even 1 minute reduces oxalate by up to 87%, significantly improving mineral absorption.
- **Trade-off**: Cooking increases antioxidant capacity but reduces some B and C vitamins. Net result: cooked has higher nutritional value.
- **Safety note**: Rich in nitrates, which can turn into toxic nitrites at high heat. Do not reheat spinach more than once — see food safety guide.

---

## Mushrooms

- **Recommended**: Cooked (grill or microwave preferred)
- **Key nutrients**: antioxidants, fiber, B vitamins, potassium, selenium, copper
- **Why cook**: Enhances antioxidant activity. Pan-frying increases absorbable protein, essential fatty acids, and carbohydrates. Raw or undercooked mushrooms can contain hazardous toxins.
- **Trade-off**: Boiling and frying reduce antioxidant levels. Pan-frying also drops protein/carb content and raises fat content.
- **Best method**: Grill or microwave to preserve the most nutrients.
- **Safety note**: Do not eat raw or undercooked mushrooms; do not store for too long before cooking.

---

## Garlic

- **Recommended**: Raw
- **Key nutrients**: allicin (active compound)
- **Why raw**: Allicin — responsible for garlic's odor and health benefits (heart health, cancer risk reduction) — is activated by an enzyme reaction when garlic is cut or crushed, and deactivated by heat.
- **If cooked**: Chop or slice garlic beforehand and wait a few minutes before adding heat. This allows allicin to develop before the enzyme is destroyed.

---

## Carrots

- **Recommended**: Cooked
- **Key nutrients**: carotenoids (beta-carotene → vitamin A), antioxidants, polyphenols, potassium
- **Why cook**: Beta-carotene absorption is 6.5x greater in stir-fried carrots than raw. Cooking breaks down cell walls and releases carotenoids.
- **Best method**: Steam for up to 10 minutes (preserves carotenoids + concentrates potassium). Boiling also preserves carotenoids but reduces polyphenols. Avoid deep frying.
- **Trade-off**: Boiling loses polyphenols; steaming cuts polyphenol losses by ~43% while unlocking carotenoids.

---

## Onions

- **Recommended**: Raw
- **Key nutrients**: thiosulfinates, allicin, flavonoids (antioxidants)
- **Why raw**: Provides thiosulfinates and allicin — antioxidants linked to cardiovascular health, immune function, and disease protection. Heat destroys these compounds, especially when onions are chopped beforehand.
- **If cooked**: Sautéing or baking at low heat for 5 minutes or less increases flavonoid levels. Boiling reduces antioxidants.

---

## Beets

- **Recommended**: Raw
- **Key nutrients**: nitrates, betalains, flavonoids
- **Why raw**: Nitrates (converted to nitrite in the body) relax blood vessels, lower blood pressure, and support immune and cardiovascular function. Betalains are antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and liver-protective pigments. Nitrates are water-soluble — boiling washes them away.
- **Safety note**: High-nitrate vegetable — do not reheat beets.

---

## Green Beans (Haricots Verts)

- **Recommended**: Cooked
- **Key nutrients**: antioxidants
- **Why cook**: Raw green beans contain lectin, an anti-nutrient that disrupts digestion and blocks absorption of calcium, iron, phosphorus, and zinc. It can cause nausea, bloating, and diarrhea. High heat or water-based cooking (boiling, stewing) inactivates lectins. Cooking also increases availability of certain antioxidants.

---

## Celery

- **Recommended**: Cooked (for antioxidants); Raw (for fiber and vitamin C)
- **Key nutrients**: antioxidants, fiber, folate, potassium, vitamins A, C, K
- **Why cook**: Boiling, pressure-cooking, baking, grilling, and frying all increase antioxidant levels. Boiling also makes folate more available.
- **Trade-off**: Cooking destroys fiber and reduces vitamin C.
- **Safety note**: High-nitrate vegetable — do not reheat celery.

---

## Kale (Chou frisé)

- **Recommended**: Raw
- **Key nutrients**: glucosinolates, vitamin C, antioxidants, calcium, potassium, iron, zinc, magnesium, fiber, vitamins A, K, B6, copper, manganese
- **Why raw**: Chopping or chewing kale releases an enzyme that converts glucosinolates into isothiocyanates — anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer compounds. Heat destroys this enzyme.
- **Trade-off**: Cooking reduces vitamin C, antioxidants, and several minerals.
- **Tip**: Massage raw kale with olive oil for a few minutes to soften before eating.
- **Note**: Same principle applies to cauliflower, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and cabbage.

---

## Tomatoes

- **Recommended**: Cooked
- **Key nutrients**: lycopene (antioxidant), vitamin C
- **Why cook**: Lycopene — linked to reduced cancer risk and cardiovascular disease protection — is more bioavailable after cooking. Heat breaks down cell walls, making it easier to absorb. Cooking can change the shape of lycopene, improving absorption further.
- **Best method**: Steaming especially increases antioxidant capacity.
- **Trade-off**: Cooking reduces vitamin C, but tomatoes are not the primary source — citrus, strawberries, and bell peppers are better alternatives.
- **Storage tip**: Store tomatoes at room temperature (not the fridge) to optimize ripening and increase lycopene levels.

---

## Asparagus

- **Recommended**: Cooked
- **Key nutrients**: ferulic acid, antioxidants (beta-carotene, quercetin)
- **Why cook**: Cooking increases total antioxidant activity by 16–25%. Beta-carotene rises by 24% and quercetin by 98%. Cooked asparagus produces 3x more antioxidants per spear than raw.
- **Tip**: Don't overcook. Thin asparagus: 1 minute. Thick spears: 4–5 minutes maximum.

---

## Broccoli

- **Recommended**: Raw (or carefully prepared before cooking)
- **Key nutrients**: glucosinolates, sulforaphane (anticancer), vitamin C
- **Why raw**: Glucosinolates are converted to sulforaphane (anti-cancer) by the enzyme myrosinase when broccoli is chewed or chopped. Heat damages this enzyme and reduces sulforaphane.
- **If cooked**: Chop into small pieces, wait at least 30–90 minutes for sulforaphane to develop, then flash stir-fry for 4 minutes. This produces 2.8x more sulforaphane than cooking immediately after chopping.
- **Note**: Steaming retains more vitamin C and glucosinolates than stir-frying.

---

## Potatoes

- **Recommended**: Cooked (then cooled for best glycemic response)
- **Key nutrients**: vitamin C, B6, folate, iron, potassium, fiber
- **Why cook**: Raw potatoes contain resistant starch that is difficult to digest and can cause gastrointestinal issues. Cooking makes them safe and digestible. Retains antioxidants regardless of cooking method.
- **Blood sugar tip**: Potatoes spike blood sugar due to high starch content. Mashing with butter/oil reduces glycemic load. Best served cooked then cooled or in a vinaigrette — this converts digestible starch to resistant starch (retrogradation), reducing glycemic load from 90 to 56%.

---

## Bell Peppers (Poivrons)

- **Recommended**: Raw
- **Key nutrients**: vitamin C, carotenoids, beneficial polyphenols
- **Why raw**: Cooking destroys up to 75% of nutrients.
- **If cooked**: Use dry-heat methods (stir-fry or roast) to minimize losses.

---

## Cauliflower

- **Recommended**: Raw
- **Key nutrients**: sulforaphane, antioxidants
- **Why raw**: Highest antioxidant losses of all vegetables observed after boiling and microwaving. Same glucosinolate/myrosinase principle as broccoli and kale applies.
- **If cooked**: Griddle or stir-fry. For maximum sulforaphane, blend or chop into rice-sized pieces and refrigerate before cooking.

---

## Eggs

- **Recommended**: Cooked
- **Key nutrients**: B vitamins, zinc, calcium, choline (100mg+), lutein, zeaxanthin, vitamin A
- **Why cook**: Protein digestibility increases from 51% (raw) to 91% (cooked). Heat unfolds protein structures, making them easier to digest. Raw eggs carry Salmonella risk and contain avidin, a protein that causes biotin deficiency.
- **Benefits**: Eggs increase HDL ("good" cholesterol), reducing risk of heart disease and stroke. Choline builds cell membranes and supports brain signaling. Lutein and zeaxanthin protect against cataracts and macular degeneration.
- **Best method**: Poach or boil to limit air exposure and maximize nutrient retention. Do not overcook.

---

## Milk

- **Raw vs pasteurized**: Regular pasteurization does not significantly change nutrient content. Ultra-high-temperature (UHT) pasteurization (135–150°C) does affect many vitamins.
- **If boiling**: Some lactose converts to lactulose (non-digestible). Some long-chain fats convert to short- and medium-chain fats (may support weight loss and gut health). B vitamins decrease slightly — but milk is not a primary source of B vitamins (except riboflavin, which is rarely deficient).
- **Tip**: Heat milk slowly over medium heat and stir while bringing to a boil.
